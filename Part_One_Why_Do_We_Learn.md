## From Grinding Through Olympiad Problems to Solving Complex Problems: **What Truly Determines How Far a Person Can Go Is Systems Thinking, Not the Number of Problems They Solve**

### The Core Question

**What is the ultimate purpose of learning?**

The fundamental goal is to move from **“solving exercises” to “solving real, complex, and unknown problems.”**

---

# From Grinding Through Olympiad Problems to Solving Complex Problems: **What Truly Determines How Far a Person Can Go Is Systems Thinking, Not the Number of Problems They Solve**

Education has a question that is often overlooked:

**Does being very good at solving problems mean that a person is good at solving problems in the real world?**

At first glance, the two seem almost identical. Solving an exercise is, after all, a form of problem-solving, and solving a large number of problems can indeed improve one's ability to solve them.

But when we broaden our perspective from a single problem or algorithm to a complex system, a research project, or even a real-world project, we discover that **the ability to solve exercises and the ability to solve complex problems are actually two different tracks.**

## 1. Practicing Problems Has Value—but That Value Has Limits

A student who practices large numbers of mathematical Olympiad problems can master many problem types, solution techniques, and ways of thinking.

For example, after solving hundreds or thousands of problems, a person may encounter a new problem and quickly recognize what category it belongs to, then find a solution from methods accumulated in the past.

**This is a very important ability.**

The same principle applies to computer science.

A newly graduated computer science student who has practiced a large number of LeetCode problems may be extremely familiar with binary trees, dynamic programming, graph algorithms, backtracking, sliding windows, and many other algorithms. They may even be able to write standard solutions very quickly during a technical interview.

However, **being able to solve LeetCode problems quickly does not mean that a person can design a high-quality software system, much less independently complete a complex engineering project.**

**Problem practice trains the ability to find correct answers within relatively clear rules and boundaries.**

**Real-world problems, however, often have no standard answer at all.**

That is the most important difference between the two.

---

## 2. Moving from “Finding the Answer” to “Defining the Problem” Is a Huge Leap

An examination problem has usually already completed the most important step for you: **problem definition**.

The problem tells you:

- What the known conditions are;
- What needs to be solved;
- What constraints exist;
- What form the final answer should take.

What you actually need to do is find a path to the answer within the defined boundaries.

But complex real-world problems are completely different.

A genuine research problem, software architecture problem, or business problem often requires you to first answer:

> **“What problem are we actually trying to solve?”**

You may even need to ask:

> **“Is this problem worth solving at all?”**

At this point, the truly important abilities are no longer about remembering how many solution techniques you know. They are about:

- Whether you can discover the real problem;
- Whether you can build the right problem model;
- Whether you can determine which factors matter most;
- Whether you can formulate reasonable hypotheses;
- Whether you can design ways to validate those hypotheses;
- Whether you can adjust your direction after failure;
- Whether you can gradually transform an ambiguous problem into one that can actually be solved.

**This is what we call systems thinking.**

---

## 3. Training for Mathematical Olympiads and Becoming a Mathematician Are Two Different Levels

Being extremely good at mathematical Olympiad problems does not directly imply that a person will eventually become an outstanding mathematician.

Extensive Olympiad training certainly has value. It can cultivate interest in mathematics, strengthen logical reasoning, and allow a person to accumulate extensive experience with mathematical problems.

But **solving competition problems and conducting original mathematical research are fundamentally different levels of activity.**

Competition problems usually ask you to find an ingenious proof or computational method within an existing mathematical framework.

True mathematical research, however, often confronts a problem for which there is no known answer.

You may not even know:

- How the problem should be defined;
- Which path is the correct one;
- Which conjectures might be true;
- Which directions are worth pursuing;
- Why previous methods have failed;
- Whether it is possible to create an entirely new theory.

Therefore, **even if a person can solve a large number of extremely difficult Olympiad problems, that does not mean they possess the ability to solve historic mathematical problems.**

**The greater the difficulty of a problem, the closer it is not necessarily to genuine research ability.**

**These are two different dimensions and cannot simply be equated.**

---

## 4. LeetCode and Real Software Engineering Are the Same

The same issue exists in the computer industry.

Many university graduates can solve large numbers of LeetCode problems and perform exceptionally well in algorithm interviews, but after joining a company, they may discover that they are not particularly good at completing a large-scale project.

Why?

Because real software engineering problems do not tell you:

> “Use algorithm X to solve this problem within O(n log n) time complexity.”

In real-world engineering, engineers must decide for themselves:

- How the system should be designed;
- How data should be organized;
- How services should communicate;
- How high concurrency should be handled;
- How reliability should be ensured;
- How failures should be recovered from;
- How to balance performance, cost, and maintainability;
- How to understand what customers actually need;
- How to turn an ambiguous requirement into a system that can operate reliably over the long term.

**This is no longer a LeetCode problem.**

**It is a systems engineering problem.**

Therefore, solving thousands of LeetCode problems does not necessarily mean that a person will write high-quality code, much less design and complete a high-quality large-scale project.

This does not mean that LeetCode has no value. Rather, we should understand its value correctly:

> **LeetCode is a training tool, not a comprehensive measure of a person's engineering ability.**

---

## 5. What Really Matters Is Knowing: **Why Am I Doing This?**

Therefore, the most important question in learning is not simply:

> “How many more problems do I need to solve?”

Instead, we should ask:

> **“What ability am I trying to develop by doing these things?”**

If a student is building foundational knowledge, solving large numbers of problems is meaningful.

If a programmer is preparing for a technical interview, practicing LeetCode also has practical value.

But if a person has already entered a higher stage of learning and still treats **“solving more problems”** as the primary way to improve, they may fall into a trap:

> **Using low-level effort to substitute for the development of higher-level abilities.**

Learning must have stages.

The foundational stage requires knowledge accumulation and extensive practice.

At a higher stage, however, a person needs to gradually move:

- From **“accepting problems” to “discovering problems”**;
- From **“searching for answers” to “creating methods.”**

A truly excellent learner should understand:

**Which activities are important in the long term, and which are important only for a particular stage.**

---

## 6. Education Should Ultimately Cultivate the Ability to Solve Unknown Problems

Traditional exam-oriented education has one obvious strength: it is very effective at training students to solve **problems that have already been defined**.

The teacher gives the problem; the student solves it.

The teacher provides the knowledge; the student memorizes it.

The teacher provides the standard answer; the student searches for the correct path.

This educational model can be extremely effective at improving foundational knowledge and examination performance.

But once students enter genuine research, engineering, and social practice, they encounter completely different kinds of problems.

**Real-world problems usually have no standard answers.**

Therefore, education should cultivate not only the ability to **“solve problems,”** but also the ability to:

**Think independently, build models, formulate hypotheses, test those hypotheses, and continuously revise one's thinking when facing unknown problems.**

**This is a higher-order ability.**

---

## 7. The Value of an Excellent Mentor Is Not to Tell You the Answer, but to Teach You How to Think

One of the biggest differences between a truly excellent teacher and an ordinary teacher is not how much knowledge the teacher possesses, but whether the teacher can help students build **their own system of thinking**.

An ordinary teaching approach might be:

> “This problem should be solved this way. Remember this method.”

A truly excellent mentor might instead say:

> **“Why do you think this direction is correct? Continue down this path and see what happens.”**

Even when a student reaches a dead end, the mentor does not immediately give them the answer. Instead, the mentor helps them analyze:

- Where exactly did the problem arise?
- Was the basic assumption wrong?
- Was the model constructed incorrectly?
- Was the direction itself wrong?
- Or was an important condition overlooked?

This approach to education is enormously valuable.

Because what the student ultimately gains is not the answer to a particular problem, but **the ability to discover errors, correct direction, and develop new methods.**

**That is a capability that can stay with a person for a lifetime.**

---

## 8. The Height of Education Depends Greatly on the Height of the Teacher

How far a student can go depends greatly on the kind of teachers and mentors they encounter.

Foundational knowledge can be acquired through textbooks, the internet, and extensive practice.

But higher-level ways of thinking are much harder to acquire simply from textbooks.

**A person can rarely teach something they have never truly mastered themselves.**

If a teacher remains at the level of:

> **“Solve the problem → find the answer → provide the answer,”**

it is difficult for that teacher to genuinely cultivate students' ability to conduct independent research and solve complex problems.

Conversely, if a mentor is working at the frontier of a research field and possesses strong systems thinking, what they can give students is not merely knowledge, but **a way of seeing and approaching problems.**

This is also why many great scientists have produced outstanding students and collaborators around them.

**A master-level teacher does not merely transfer knowledge. More importantly, they transfer a way of thinking.**

---

## 9. From “Individual Ability” to “Collaborative Ability”

Modern society has another frequently overlooked reality:

**Complex problems are almost never solved by one person alone.**

A large-scale software system requires collaboration among product managers, architects, developers, testers, operations engineers, and many other roles.

A large research project requires scientists from different disciplines to work together.

A technology company needs engineers, scientists, product managers, leaders, and business teams to work together toward a common goal.

Therefore, modern education must not only cultivate an individual's ability to solve problems. It must also cultivate:

- Communication skills;
- Collaboration skills;
- Cross-disciplinary abilities;
- Organizational skills;
- Leadership skills;
- The ability to integrate knowledge from different people.

The old evaluation system of:

> **“One person gets every problem right, therefore that person is excellent,”**

is clearly insufficient when dealing with complex social problems.

**Truly complex problems require not only smart people, but systems that enable different smart people to work together.**

---

## 10. True Education Is Ultimately About Becoming Independent of Standard Answers

In the early stages of learning, a person needs standard answers.

They need them because they must first build a foundation of knowledge.

But the ultimate goal of learning should not be to remain forever focused on finding standard answers.

Advanced learning is a progression:

> **“Other people tell me what the problem is.”**

Then:

> **“I can determine which problems are worth solving.”**

And eventually:

> **“I can formulate questions that no one has asked before and create methods to solve them.”**

This is the process of moving from **student to researcher, engineer, scientist, and ultimately thinker.**

Therefore, we cannot simply judge a person's ultimate potential by:

- How many problems they have solved;
- How many LeetCode problems they have completed;
- How many points they have earned on exams.

These metrics can measure certain abilities, but **they cannot measure the entirety of a person's potential.**

What truly determines how far a person can ultimately go is whether they can grow from:

> **“Solving problems defined by others”**

into:

> **“Defining problems themselves and solving unknown problems.”**

---

# Conclusion: **Learning Is Not About Having More Answers; It Is About Gaining the Ability to Create Answers**

Solving problems is not wrong.

Examinations are not wrong.

Mathematical Olympiad training has value, and LeetCode training has value as well.

**The real problem is that we must not mistake the tool for the goal, nor mistake a stage-specific ability for the ultimate ability.**

Solving problems can help a person build a foundation.

Practicing algorithm problems can help a person prepare for interviews.

Memorizing knowledge can help a person establish a cognitive framework.

But as a person moves to a higher level, they must gradually transcend these forms of training.

They need to learn to think independently.

They need to learn to analyze problems systematically.

They need to learn to face problems without standard answers.

They need to learn to correct their direction through failure.

They need to learn to collaborate with others to solve complex problems.

Ultimately, the most important goal of education should not be to create the **“person who is best at solving problems.”**

Instead, it should cultivate a person who, when facing an unknown world, can still:

**Discover problems, understand problems, define problems, solve problems—and even create new problems and new methods.**

**That is the true meaning of learning.**

And **this is the critical step in a person's journey from being an “excellent student” to becoming a truly outstanding human being.**

---

# But Recognizing the Goal of Learning Is Not Enough

Once we recognize that the ultimate goal of learning is not simply to solve more problems or memorize more knowledge, but to develop **systems thinking and the ability to solve complex problems**, a more practical question naturally emerges:

> **How should a person learn in order to truly develop these abilities?**

If someone spends ten hours a day studying but merely solves problems mechanically, memorizes information, and repeats the same exercises, they may spend a great deal of time learning without actually developing as much ability as they imagine.

Conversely, some people may not study for very long, yet through effective learning methods they can quickly understand knowledge, build connections among different concepts, and transfer what they have learned to new problems.

This leads to another very important question:

> **What is genuine learning efficiency?**

Learning efficiency should not simply be understood as:

> **“How many problems can I solve per unit of time?”**

Instead, it should be understood as:

> **“How much transferable, applicable, and creative ability can I genuinely develop per unit of time?”**

Therefore, recognizing **what abilities should be cultivated** is only the first step.

The next question is:

> **How can we use more effective learning methods to achieve the greatest possible improvement in our abilities within limited time?**

This leads directly to the true meaning of **learning efficiency**, which will be the next issue we need to explore in depth.

**Truly effective learning is not about enabling a person to finish more problems faster. It is about enabling a person to move faster from “knowing the answer” to “understanding the principle,” from “understanding the principle” to “being able to solve new problems,” and ultimately to “being able to create their own methods.”**

**And perhaps this is one of the most important questions worth studying in education and learning.**
